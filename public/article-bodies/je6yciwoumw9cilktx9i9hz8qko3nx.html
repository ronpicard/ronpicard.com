As computational power increases, deep neural networks provide an empirically effective way to classify large data sets based on a small labeled subset. Forest-type covers are an example application. It is imperative for private and public land management agencies to have accurate records of forest cover for the regions under their management, but legal and manpower limitations mean this is not always possible. Neural networks provide a mechanism by which this data may be obtained.

This paper presents an improvement on feed-forward neural networks used to classify forest cover types from cartographic features. We keep the data and train/test split of an earlier study by Blackard et al., whose single-hidden-layer network scored 70.58%, and ask how far deeper, wider, and better-regularized networks can go.

The data

The data is the UCI Covertype set: 581,012 samples, each a 30 by 30 meter cell of Roosevelt National Forest in northern Colorado, in areas with little direct human management. Each cell has 54 features: 10 numeric ones (elevation, aspect, slope, distances to water, roadways, and wildfire ignition points, and three hillshade indices), 4 binary wilderness-area flags, and 40 binary soil-type flags. The label is one of seven cover types, from Lodgepole Pine and Spruce/Fir to Aspen and Krummholz.

We standardize the 10 numeric features (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) and leave the 44 binary features alone. Labels are one-hot encoded over the 7 classes. Following the earlier study, the split is class-balanced: 11,340 training cells (1,620 per class), 3,780 validation cells (540 per class), and 569,672 test cells, as given in the paper's table. Training on about 2% of the data and testing on the rest makes this a hard test of generalization.

The networks

All networks take the 54 inputs and end in a 7-way softmax. Compared with the earlier study, we use ReLU hidden units instead of sigmoid, the Adam optimizer instead of plain gradient descent with momentum, dropout of 0.25 on every hidden layer, and deeper and wider architectures, with optional L1 or L2 weight penalties. Every trial uses minibatches of 512, and Adam uses a learning rate of 0.001, momentum 0.9, and RMSProp decay 0.999.

The L1 and L2 strengths, 0.00001 each, were chosen because stronger values stopped the network from fitting and weaker ones did little. We expected L1 to help, since it pushes weights to zero and could sparsify the 44 binary inputs, and L2 to hurt.

Results

Every trial beats the earlier 70.58%, with test accuracies from 74.04% to 76.57%. The best is Trial 3, the 54-1024-512-216-7 network with dropout alone, at 76.57%, nearly six points above the earlier result.

Adding a second hidden layer in Trial 2 did not help (74.04% against 74.70%), so the extra capacity was not the limit for small networks. The wide network of Trial 3 did help. The regularizers did not: L1 in Trial 4 cost about a point (75.47%) and L2 in Trial 5 cost more (74.30%), with a final cost of 1.6036 against 0.0004 for Trial 3.

Overfitting

The networks fit their 11,340 training cells almost perfectly while generalizing much less well. The loss curves show the same pattern: the small networks of Trials 1 and 2 stay noisy and never settle, while the wide networks of Trials 3 to 5 drop to near zero within about 5,000 steps.

Conclusions

Deeper and wider networks have more capacity and fit the training data better, and all five beat the earlier single-hidden-layer network. The effect of L1 was small and L2 made things worse. Given this much overfitting, there is little more to gain with such a small training subset; the natural next steps are to use far more of the 581,012 samples for training and to tune network capacity with a hypothesis-testing approach rather than by trial and error.

A note from 2026

The comparison to the earlier study changes several things at once (activation, optimizer, dropout, depth), so the gain is not attributed to any one of them. Each trial was run once, and the shallow networks trained for 10,000 epochs against 2,000 for the deep ones, so that comparison is imperfect. The paper's text also gives Trial 3 the figures of Trial 4 in its conclusion; the table above is the correct record.
