In this post we train the same multi-layer network as in the momentum post, again as logistic regression, but with minibatch stochastic gradient descent and Adaptive Moments, usually written Adam. The question is whether Adam, which adapts the step size of every weight, beats momentum on the same problem.

The task and network

The task is the same: classify MNIST digits as 9 (label 1) or not 9 (label 0). Images are flattened to 784 values and scaled by 1/255, and we train on the first 50,000 images and test on the next 10,000, reshuffling every epoch. The network is the 784, 20, 10, 1 network from the momentum post: two ReLU hidden layers and a sigmoid output.

What Adam adds

The momentum post gave every weight the same learning rate and added a velocity. Adam keeps two running averages for each weight: a moving average of the gradient (the first moment, which is momentum) and a moving average of the squared gradient (the second moment, which is the idea behind RMSProp). It then divides one by the square root of the other, so a weight with consistently large gradients takes smaller steps and a weight with small gradients takes relatively larger ones. With g the gradient and t the step count:

m ← β₁ · m + (1 − β₁) · g
v ← β₂ · v + (1 − β₂) · g²
m̂ = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ), v̂ = v / (1 − β₂ᵗ)
w ← w − α · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

The corrections 1 / (1 − βᵗ) matter early on, when the averages start at zero and are biased toward zero. In the notebook ε is 1e-6.

Training setup

We use a batch of 50 images and a learning rate of 0.01 for about 500 epochs, and we vary the two coefficients. Each weight matrix has a first-moment and a second-moment array of the same shape, all starting at zero.

V_dW[layer] = np.multiply(momentum_coef, V_dW[layer]) + np.multiply(1-momentum_coef, _dW)
R_dW[layer] = np.multiply(RMSProp_coef, R_dW[layer]) + np.multiply(1-RMSProp_coef, np.multiply(_dW, _dW))

t = t + 1
V_dW[layer] = np.multiply(V_dW[layer], 1/(1-np.power(momentum_coef, t)))
R_dW[layer] = np.multiply(R_dW[layer], 1/(1-np.power(RMSProp_coef, t)))

val1 = 1/(np.sqrt(R_dW[layer])+ epsilon)
W[layer] = W[layer] - np.multiply(alpha, np.multiply(V_dW[layer], val1 ))

Results

We ran three settings of (β₁, β₂) on the 50,000 images, and measured accuracy on the 10,000 test images.

  • β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.9: 98.36% in about 1,264 s.
  • β₁ = 0.5, β₂ = 0.9: 98.94% in about 1,049 s, the best of the three.
  • β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.5: 90.39% in about 1,449 s.

The first two runs show that a lighter momentum term (β₁ = 0.5) did a little better here, with the caveat that each setting ran once, and identical settings took noticeably different times, so the times are only roughly comparable.

The third run deserves a closer look. The original write-up read it as overfitting, but 90.39% is exactly 100% minus the 9.61% of test images that are nines, which is the score of a network that answers "not a 9" every time. Its cost also sat on a plateau near 0.23 throughout. Rather than overfitting, the network collapsed to a single constant answer, probably because a small β₂ makes the second-moment estimate jumpy and the steps unstable.

A note from 2026

Looking back, our Adam code departs from the standard algorithm in two ways. The bias-corrected moments were written back into the stored arrays, so the correction compounds on every step instead of being applied only to the update, and the step counter t was increased once per layer, three times per update, rather than once per update. That is a likely reason these results, at best 98.94%, trail the roughly 99.2% that momentum reached in the previous post. The reported zero costs are also partly an artefact of saturated outputs inside the log, so we would not read them as perfect fits.

Takeaway

Adam trained this network to about 98.4% to 98.9% accuracy, but in this implementation it did not beat momentum, and a poor choice of β₂ made it collapse to the majority answer. The full notebook, with every cell and plot, is on GitHub.
