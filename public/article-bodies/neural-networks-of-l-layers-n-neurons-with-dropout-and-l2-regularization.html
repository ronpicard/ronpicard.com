In this post we walk through the design, training, and testing of a neural network with multiple hidden layers, written by hand in NumPy, and then see how two regularisation techniques, L2 weight penalties and dropout, change what it learns. The network is used for logistic regression, an older name for binary classification.

The task is deliberately small so the effects are easy to see: classify points from two interleaving half circles. We judge each network by its test accuracy and by the decision boundary it draws, because a boundary that bends around individual points is the clearest sign of overfitting.

The data

The data is randomly generated: points along one half circle are labeled 0 and points along the other are labeled 1. We draw 200 points for training and a second, fresh set of 200 points for testing. The data is not normalised. The generator itself lives in a helper module that is not part of the published notebook, so we do not state its noise settings here.

As a baseline we fit scikit-learn's cross-validated logistic regression. Its boundary is a straight line, which cannot separate two interleaving moons, and it scores 84% on both the training and test sets. That is underfitting, and it is the reason we need a non-linear network.

The network and its math

Every run uses the same architecture: 2 inputs, a hidden layer of 20 ReLU units, a hidden layer of 10 ReLU units, and one sigmoid output that gives the probability that a point has label 1. Each layer computes a linear step followed by an activation:

Z⁽ˡ⁾ = W⁽ˡ⁾ · A⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾ + B⁽ˡ⁾
A⁽ˡ⁾ = max(Z⁽ˡ⁾, 0) (hidden layers), A⁽ⁿ⁾ = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ) (output)

The loss for one point is the binary cross-entropy, and the cost is its mean over the m points:

L = −y · log(a) − (1 − y) · log(1 − a)
J = (1/m) · Σ L

Backpropagation starts at the output with a clean result, dZ⁽ⁿ⁾ = A⁽ⁿ⁾ − Y, and moves backward through each hidden layer, multiplying by the ReLU derivative (1 where Z > 0, otherwise 0). Each weight matrix then takes a gradient descent step:

W ← W − α · ∂J/∂W
B ← B − α · ∂J/∂B

Two ways to regularise

L2 regularisation adds a penalty on the size of the weights to the cost, scaled by a strength λ (called epsilon in the notebook). Large weights are what let a network bend its boundary sharply around individual points, so penalising them smooths the boundary:

J = (1/m) · Σ L + (λ / 2m) · Σ W²
∂J/∂W ← ∂J/∂W + (λ / m) · W

Dropout works differently. On every training iteration we randomly switch off a fraction of the units, each kept with probability keep_prob, so no unit can rely on any particular neighbour. At test time all units are used. The notebook multiplies the activations by a random 0/1 mask and does not rescale by 1 / keep_prob, and it also masks the output layer.

if(Dropout == True):
    _D = np.float64(np.where(np.random.uniform(0, 1, [len(W[layer][:]), m]) < keep_prob, 1, 0))
    D.append(_D)
    A = np.multiply(A, _D)

Training setup

All five runs use plain gradient descent on the full batch of 200 points, with a learning rate of 0.01 for 850,000 iterations. Weights and biases start uniform in (−0.99, 0.99), and the random seed is reset before each run, so every network starts from identical weights and the only difference between runs is the regularisation. We tried no regularisation, L2 with λ = 0.85 and 0.10, and dropout with keep probabilities of 0.85 and 0.95.

What the networks learned

Without regularisation the network fits the training data very closely: its cost falls to 0.107 and its boundary wraps around individual points. Regularised runs trade a little training fit for a smoother boundary.

The learning curves look very different between methods, and the reasons matter when reading them.

How much do the differences matter?

The unregularised network reaches 88.5% and the best regularised run reaches 89.5%. On 200 test points, one point of accuracy is two test points, so the spread among all five networks is at most two points. We would not name a winner from that. What the experiments do show is the direction of the effect: the regularised networks draw smoother boundaries, the training cost behaves as each method predicts, and the strength of the penalty or the keep probability changes how much smoothing we get.

A note from 2026

The dropout here is not the usual inverted dropout: the activations are not rescaled by 1 / keep_prob, so the scale of the signal differs between training and testing, and the mask was also applied to the output layer, which is normally left alone. The L2 code is also hard-coded to a three-layer network. With test accuracies this close, a larger test set and several random seeds would be needed to say which method is better.

Takeaway

A small ReLU network separates the two moons far better than logistic regression, and both L2 and dropout visibly smooth its boundary. The full notebook, with every cell and plot, is on GitHub.
